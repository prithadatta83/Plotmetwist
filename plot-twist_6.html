It became one of the most replayed clips in the whole nepotism war: Sonam Kapoor, sitting across from Rajkumar Rao, arguing that a star kid's only real edge is the network, and it comes loaded with expectation.

Rajkummar — an outsider who clawed in from Gurgaon with no one waiting for him — was blunter: that it's grating to watch weak actors keep landing film after film, twenty-five deep into a career, on the strength of whose son or daughter they are.

Two people, opposite engines, one argument that never resolves. So this first issue of Plot.me.twist tries to settle it the boring way — with simple maths. I pulled every release I could find, identified the main cast for each, and split them into two camps: actors from generational movie families (star kids) and everyone else (outsiders). Then I asked the data which of them was closer to right. Here goes.

Fact 01

A skew you cannot miss

Start with Sonam's claim that the network is the one real edge. If she's right, it should leave a fingerprint — and it does. Star kids are 3.0% of the talent pool — 84 of 2,792 actors. If casting were blind, they'd appear in roughly 3% of films. Instead they headline 34% of them — 960 of 2,824 films, an eleven-fold over-representation. That single gap is the network made visible, drawn in one line.

Fact 02

Fading, but far from gone

The tide is going out — slowly. Total releases have climbed (the bars), but the star-kid share (the line) has slid from 45% in 2000, and a 55% peak in 2002, down to about 34% — bottoming out at a 19% trough in 2020.

Why 2020 cratered to 19% The pandemic shut theatres. The big star-kid tentpoles were postponed to 2021–22, while the films that did release skewed small and OTT-first — territory outsiders dominate. So the share fell to its lowest point on record, then bounced straight back up as theatres reopened.

Fact 03

The one-and-done gap

Here's the finding that reframed the whole question for me. The nepotism edge isn't really about landing more roles than the top stars — it's about getting a second one. So picture two rooms of 100 debutants each:

A true one-and-done is almost unheard of for a star kid. Even the shortest star-kid careers in the data still ran several films — Faisal Khan (5 films), Tanishaa Mukerji (5). The floor for them is a handful of tries. For an outsider, the floor is usually film one.

“Outsiders mostly get one chance. Star kids get many.”— The clearest line in the data
Fact 04

But does a star kid guarantee box-office success?

The obvious test is money. Where we can see it, the gap is stark — in 2023, star-kid films averaged ₹243 cr against the outsiders' ₹61 cr, a 4.0× chasm. But the box office turns out to be the wrong court to settle this in.

The catch Missing gross is heavily concentrated in non-nepo films, so the data is far from missing-at-random — an outsider film is 2.2× as likely to have no recorded box office (69% of outsider titles have no gross on record, against 32% of star-kid ones). That's intuitive: nepo-cast films get wider theatrical releases and more press, so their gross gets reported; smaller outsider films (and OTT titles) often have no published number at all. More than half the gross is missing, and missing in a biased way. So I can't crown a winner on money — I need a metric that's actually filled in.
Fact 05

Who grabs more eyeballs?

IMDb gives two near-complete columns: a film's rating and its vote count. A rating is just an opinion. The number of votes is closer to a turnstile count — how many people actually showed up and bothered to weigh in. And it's the votes, not the rating, that track the money.

Fact 06

The career trajectory

Now the headline chart. Track the average reach of each camp by where a film sits in an actor's career — their 1st film, 2nd, 3rd, and so on. The star-kid line sits above the outsider line at almost every position, and the gap is there from film one. Star kids don't just end up bigger; they start bigger and keep compounding.

Case Study

Sonam & Rajkummar

Which brings us back to the two people in that interview. Two careers, opposite engines. Sonam Kapoor's advantage is front-loaded: a famous-family debut and one breakout year (Raanjhanaa and Bhaag Milkha Bhaag, both 2013). The Plot Twist

So, who's right?

Both of them landed a piece of it. Sonam was right that the edge is the network, and right that it doesn't guarantee greatness — star kids don't blow up more often than the best outsiders; their reach grows modestly, steadily, unremarkably. Nepotism doesn't manufacture superstars. On that, the data is firmly on her side.

But Rajkummar was pointing at something real too. What the network manufactures is a floor. The privilege isn't the debut everyone fights about — it's the second film, and the fifth, and the twenty-fifth, and the quiet removal of the downside. His grievance about weak actors stacking up twenty-five credits isn't bitterness; it's the one-and-done gap in plain language. An outsider gets one chance and a 64% shot at vanishing. A star kid gets a runway. That's why an outsider needs a Queen, a Bhaag Milkha Bhaag, a once-in-a-career breakout just to draw level — and roughly twenty films to do it, if they're lucky enough to get twenty.

The hopeful twist: the door is opening. The star-kid share has fallen from its 55% peak to the mid-30s, OTT has handed outsiders a second stage, and the lifetime top tier is stacked with outsiders — Akshay, Shah Rukh, Deepika, Nawazuddin Siddiqui. The ceiling is reachable. It's just a longer, steeper climb — and you have to survive your first film to start it.

So is the banyan tree a fair metaphor? Half of one. The expectation Sonam described is real — the comparisons, the borrowed image, the public daring you to fail. But the data says it's the cheaper side of the trade. Nothing grows in the shade, maybe; nothing dies there either. The downside an outsider faces is extinction after film one. The downside a star kid faces is being underrated. Those aren't the same weather.

The data won't end the Twitter wars. But it does reframe the question. Nepotism doesn't hand you stardom. It hands you runway. What you do with the runway is on you.
