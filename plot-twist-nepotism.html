Plot Twist · No. 12000–2025

Nepotism in Bollywood,
by the Numbers

Of 3,425 actors this century, just 79 were star kids — 2.3%. They headline one in three films. Here is what a quarter-century of data actually says about who gets the spotlight.

2,824 films · 12,581 appearances · 26 years

Growing up in the '90s was all about the big American dream, the economy cracking open — and most importantly, Raveena Tandon dancing to "Tu Cheez Badi Hai Mast Mast."

So it figures that in 2020 the word "nepotism" was reborn in the middle of a global pandemic — and that the debate has stayed open ever since, with no conclusion, flaring up on social media whenever an actor-turned-politician sends a reminder alert and the other side rushes to defend itself.

So here's me, once again trying to settle my own questions with data. I pulled every release I could find, identified the main cast for each, and split them into two camps: actors from generational movie families (star kids) and everyone else (outsiders). Here goes.

Fact 01

A skew you cannot miss

Star kids are 2.3% of the talent pool. If casting were blind, they'd appear in roughly 2.3% of films. Instead they headline 33% of them — a fourteen-fold over-representation. That single gap is the whole debate, drawn in one line.

Fact 02

Fading, but far from gone

The tide is going out — slowly. Total releases have climbed, but the star-kid share has drifted down from a 53% peak in 2006 to the low 30s, bottoming out at a 19% trough in 2020.

Why 2020 cratered to 19% The pandemic shut theatres. The big star-kid tentpoles were postponed to 2021–22, while the films that did release skewed small and OTT-first — territory outsiders dominate. So the share fell to its lowest point on record, then bounced straight back up as theatres reopened.
Fact 03

The one-and-done gap

Here's the finding that reframed the whole question for me. The nepotism edge isn't really about landing more roles than the top stars — it's about getting a second one. 63 of 100 outsiders vanish after their first film. Among star kids, it's barely 14 of 100.

A true one-and-done is almost unheard of for a star kid. Even the shortest star-kid careers in the data still ran several films — Faisal Khan (5 films), Tanishaa Mukerji (6). The floor for them is a handful of tries. For an outsider, the floor is usually film one.

"Outsiders mostly get one chance. Star kids get many."— The clearest line in the data
Fact 04

But does a star kid guarantee box-office success?

The obvious test is money. Where we can see it, the gap is stark — in 2023, star-kid films averaged ₹197 cr against the outsiders' ₹42 cr, a 4.7× chasm. But the box office turns out to be the wrong court to settle this in.

The catch Missing gross is heavily concentrated in non-nepo films, so the data is far from missing-at-random — an outsider film is more than twice as likely to have no recorded box office. More than half the gross is missing, and missing in a biased way. So I can't crown a winner on money — I need a metric that's actually filled in.
Fact 05

Who grabs more eyeballs?

IMDb gives two near-complete columns: a film's rating and its vote count. A rating is just an opinion. The number of votes is closer to a turnstile count — how many people actually showed up and bothered to weigh in. And it's the votes, not the rating, that track the money (correlation ≈ 0.7 vs ≈ 0.2).

Fact 06

The career trajectory

Track the average reach of each camp by where a film sits in an actor's career — their 1st film, 2nd, 3rd, and so on. The star-kid line sits above the outsider line at almost every position, and the gap is there from film one. Star kids don't just end up bigger; they start bigger and keep compounding.

…but the ceiling is reachable

23 outsiders accumulated enough lifetime reach to crash the star-kid top tier — Akshay Kumar, Shah Rukh Khan, Anupam Kher, Boman Irani, Ajay Devgn, Deepika Padukone, Paresh Rawal, Amitabh Bachchan, Katrina Kaif, R. Madhavan, Tabu, Mithun Chakraborty, Nawazuddin Siddiqui, Ranveer Singh, Jackie Shroff, Sharman Joshi, Mona Singh, Pallavi Joshi, John Abraham, Pankaj Tripathi, Sushant Singh Rajput, Kriti Sanon, Darshan Kumar. Most of them needed 50–100 films to get there. The door opens for outsiders — it's just a much longer hallway.

Case Study

Sonam & Rajkummar

Two careers, opposite engines. Sonam Kapoor's advantage is front-loaded: a famous-family debut and one breakout year (Raanjhanaa and Bhaag Milkha Bhaag, both 2013). Rajkummar Rao crawls along the floor for years — until 2014, when Queen detonates and his curve goes vertical. The star kid never had to find a Queen; she began where he arrives only after a miracle.

The Plot Twist

So, who's right?

Both sides, a little. The loudest claim — that nepotism guarantees stardom — doesn't survive the data. Star kids don't blow up more often than the best outsiders; their reach grows modestly, steadily, unremarkably. Nepotism doesn't manufacture superstars.

What it manufactures is a floor. The real privilege isn't the debut everyone fights about — it's the second film, and the third, and the quiet removal of the downside. An outsider gets one chance and a 63% shot at vanishing. A star kid gets a runway.

The data won't end the Twitter wars. But it does reframe the question. Nepotism doesn't hand you stardom. It hands you runway. What you do with the runway is on you.
